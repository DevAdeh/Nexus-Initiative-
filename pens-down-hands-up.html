Pens Down, Hands Up

How school abductions are turning classrooms into places of fear for children in Nigeria

On the morning of 15 May 2026, armed men on motorcycles attacked three schools in Ahoro-Esiele and Yawota, two farming communities in Oriire, Oyo State. They went from classroom to classroom. When they left, 39 pupils and seven teachers had been taken into the forest.

It was the first large-scale abduction from schools in southwest Nigeria. Until then, many people in the region thought of such attacks as a northern problem.

Oriire was not the only attack that day. In Mussa, Borno State, more than 40 pupils were taken from a primary and junior secondary school. Authorities linked the Oyo attackers to Ansaru, a Boko Haram splinter group, which demanded the release of detained commanders. The Borno attack was blamed on Boko Haram. The aim is the same in both cases. A school holds dozens of children in one place, often with no fence and no guard, and each child becomes leverage against the government.

Nigeria has seen this before. In 2014, 276 girls were taken from Chibok. In 2020, hundreds of boys were taken from Kankara. Nigeria endorsed the Safe Schools Declaration in 2015, yet many rural schools still have no perimeter wall, no trained guard and no quick way to call for help.

After the headlines fade, the fear stays. Some parents keep their children at home. Pupils who were not taken worry that their school is next. Teachers struggle to teach when they are not sure of their own safety. Lessons stop, and children lose months or years of schooling.

“Pens down” describes that interruption to learning. “Hands up” describes the helplessness of people who cannot defend themselves.

Reopening school gates will not be enough to bring children back. Three things need to happen. Security agencies must set up faster, intelligence-led responses for rural schools. Governments must support early-warning systems that let residents, teachers and local leaders report suspicious movements before an attack. And pupils and teachers who return must get trauma support. A rescue that ignores emotional recovery is only half finished.

A Safe Schools policy should be judged by how many children can go to school without fearing for their lives, not by how many commitments have been announced. The goal is to get children back at their desks, learning without fear.
